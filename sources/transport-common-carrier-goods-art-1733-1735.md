# Source text
Article 1733. Common carriers, from the nature of their business and for reasons of public policy, are bound to observe extraordinary diligence in the vigilance over the goods and for the safety of the passengers transported by them, according to all the circumstances of each case.

Article 1734. Common carriers are responsible for the loss, destruction, or deterioration of the goods, unless the same is due to any of the following causes only: (1) Flood, storm, earthquake, lightning, or other natural disaster or calamity; (2) Act of the public enemy in war, whether international or civil; (3) Act or omission of the shipper or owner of the goods; (4) The character of the goods or defects in the packing or in the containers; (5) Order or act of competent public authority.

Article 1735. In all cases other than those mentioned in Nos. 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 of the preceding article, if the goods are lost, destroyed or deteriorated, common carriers are presumed to have been at fault or to have acted negligently, unless they prove that they observed extraordinary diligence as required in article 1733.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- Common carriers must observe extraordinary diligence over the goods they carry (Art. 1733).
- They are liable for loss, destruction or deterioration of goods unless it was due ONLY to one of the five causes in Art. 1734.
- Outside those five causes, the carrier is presumed at fault or negligent, unless it proves it observed extraordinary diligence (Art. 1735).
- The shipper does not have to prove the carrier's negligence; proof of loss or damage in transit is enough to raise the presumption.

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: A trucking company carried 100 sacks of rice. On the way, its driver stopped to eat and left the truck unlocked, and 20 sacks were stolen by unknown persons. The carrier says theft is not its fault. Is it liable?
Model answer: Yes. Theft by ordinary thieves is not one of the five causes in Art. 1734, so under Art. 1735 the carrier is presumed negligent. It can escape only by proving extraordinary diligence under Art. 1733, and leaving the loaded truck unlocked is the opposite of extraordinary diligence. The carrier is liable for the 20 sacks.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Original bar-style problem. A related issue was asked in the 2025 Bar Examination, Commercial Law.) Bilis Trucking, which hauls cargo for anyone who hires it, agreed to carry 500 bags of cement from Bulacan to Pampanga. On the way, an ordinary afternoon rain fell, and water seeped through a torn tarpaulin covering the cargo. Half the bags hardened and became useless. The shipper sued for the loss. Bilis Trucking argued that rain is a natural phenomenon and that the shipper must prove the company was negligent. Is Bilis Trucking liable? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: Yes. Bilis Trucking is liable for the damaged cement.
- Legal basis: A common carrier must observe extraordinary diligence in the vigilance over the goods (Art. 1733). It is liable for loss, destruction or deterioration of the goods unless caused only by one of the five causes in Art. 1734, such as flood, storm or other natural disaster or calamity. In all other cases, the carrier is presumed at fault or negligent unless it proves extraordinary diligence (Art. 1735).
- Application: Bilis Trucking offers its service to anyone, so it is a common carrier. An ordinary afternoon rain is not a flood, storm or other natural disaster or calamity under Art. 1734(1). Since the damage does not fall under any of the five causes, Bilis Trucking is presumed negligent; the shipper does not have to prove negligence. Far from showing extraordinary diligence, the torn tarpaulin shows the carrier failed to protect the cargo.
- Conclusion: Bilis Trucking cannot overcome the presumption of negligence under Art. 1735, so it is liable to the shipper for the damaged cement.

# Objective and multiple-choice quiz (Year 1-2)
Q (multiple choice): What degree of diligence must a common carrier observe over the goods it carries (Art. 1733)?
A) Ordinary diligence
B) Diligence of a good father of a family
C) Extraordinary diligence
D) Slight diligence
Correct: C
Hint: Art. 1733 says it is required "from the nature of their business and for reasons of public policy".
Hint: It is the highest degree of diligence.
Why: Art. 1733 requires common carriers to observe extraordinary diligence in the vigilance over goods and the safety of passengers.

Q (multiple choice): Which is one of the five causes in Art. 1734 that frees a common carrier from liability for lost goods?
A) Theft by ordinary thieves
B) A mechanical breakdown of the truck
C) Act or omission of the shipper or owner of the goods
D) The driver's mistake
Correct: C
Hint: Art. 1734 lists natural disasters, war, the shipper's own act, the goods' character or packing, and acts of public authority.
Hint: Look for the cause that comes from the owner of the goods.
Why: Art. 1734(3) lists the act or omission of the shipper or owner of the goods.

Q (objective): Under Art. 1735, if goods are lost for a cause not listed in Art. 1734, the common carrier is ______ to have been at fault. (One word.)
Accept: presumed
Hint: The shipper doesn't have to prove the carrier's negligence.
Why: Art. 1735 presumes the carrier at fault or negligent unless it proves extraordinary diligence.

Q (objective): How many exempting causes does Art. 1734 list? (Give the number.)
Accept: 5 | five
Hint: Count the numbered items in Art. 1734.
Why: Art. 1734 lists five causes only.
