# Source text
Article 1767. By the contract of partnership two or more persons bind themselves to contribute money, property, or industry to a common fund, with the intention of dividing the profits among themselves. Two or more persons may also form a partnership for the exercise of a profession.

Article 1769. In determining whether a partnership exists, these rules shall apply: (1) Except as provided by article 1825, persons who are not partners as to each other are not partners as to third persons; (2) Co-ownership or co-possession does not of itself establish a partnership, whether such-co-owners or co-possessors do or do not share any profits made by the use of the property; (3) The sharing of gross returns does not of itself establish a partnership, whether or not the persons sharing them have a joint or common right or interest in any property from which the returns are derived; (4) The receipt by a person of a share of the profits of a business is prima facie evidence that he is a partner in the business, but no such inference shall be drawn if such profits were received in payment: (a) As a debt by installments or otherwise; (b) As wages of an employee or rent to a landlord; (c) As an annuity to a widow or representative of a deceased partner; (d) As interest on a loan, though the amount of payment vary with the profits of the business; (e) As the consideration for the sale of a goodwill of a business or other property by installments or otherwise.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- A partnership exists when two or more persons contribute money, property or industry to a common fund, intending to divide the profits among themselves (Art. 1767).
- Co-ownership or co-possession does not, by itself, create a partnership (Art. 1769(2)).
- Sharing gross returns does not, by itself, create a partnership (Art. 1769(3)).
- Receiving a share of the profits is prima facie evidence of being a partner (Art. 1769(4)).
- But no partnership is inferred if the profit share is received as payment of a debt, wages or rent, an annuity, interest on a loan, or the price of goodwill or property (Art. 1769(4)(a)-(e)).

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: Two sisters inherited a beach lot and rent it out to tourists, splitting the rent. A supplier of one sister claims the other sister is her business partner and must pay her debts. Is there a partnership?
Model answer: No. Under Art. 1769(2), co-ownership or co-possession does not of itself establish a partnership, even if the co-owners share the profits from using the property. The sisters merely co-own the inherited lot and share its rent, so there is no partnership and the other sister is not liable as a partner.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Original bar-style problem.) Edu runs a small carinderia. To buy new stoves, he borrowed P300,000 from his friend Dina. They agreed in writing that Edu would repay the loan by giving Dina 10% of the carinderia's net profits every month until the P300,000 is fully paid. Dina had no say in running the business. When the carinderia failed, Edu's suppliers sued Dina, claiming that because she received a share of the profits, she is Edu's partner and liable for the business debts. Is Dina a partner? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: No. Dina is not a partner of Edu and is not liable for the carinderia's debts as a partner.
- Legal basis: Under Art. 1767, a partnership requires a contribution to a common fund with the intention of dividing the profits. Under Art. 1769(4), receiving a share of the profits of a business is prima facie evidence of being a partner, but no such inference may be drawn if the profits were received in payment of a debt by installments or otherwise (Art. 1769(4)(a)).
- Application: Dina received 10% of the net profits only as the way Edu repaid her P300,000 loan, until it is fully paid. That is payment of a debt by installments, the very case in which Art. 1769(4)(a) forbids inferring a partnership. Dina also had no role in the business and there was no intention to divide profits as partners.
- Conclusion: Because Dina's profit share was payment of a loan, she is a creditor, not a partner, and the suppliers cannot hold her liable as a partner.

# Objective and multiple-choice quiz (Year 1-2)
Q (multiple choice): Under Art. 1767, partners contribute money, property or industry to a common fund with the intention of:
A) avoiding taxes
B) dividing the profits among themselves
C) lending money to the public
D) owning property in common
Correct: B
Hint: Read the end of the first sentence of Art. 1767.
Hint: It is the purpose that makes it a business partnership.
Why: Art. 1767 requires a contribution to a common fund with the intention of dividing the profits among the partners.

Q (multiple choice): Under Art. 1769, receiving a share of a business's profits is prima facie evidence of being a partner, EXCEPT when the share is received as:
A) a reward for being a partner
B) wages of an employee
C) the partner's capital return
D) a share in the partnership's profits
Correct: B
Hint: Art. 1769(4) lists five exceptions, (a) to (e).
Hint: One of them is pay for work done as an employee.
Why: Under Art. 1769(4)(b), no partnership is inferred if the profit share is received as wages of an employee or rent to a landlord.

Q (objective): Under Art. 1769(3), the sharing of gross ______ does not of itself establish a partnership. (One word.)
Accept: returns | gross returns
Hint: It is the total income before expenses.
Why: Art. 1769(3) says the sharing of gross returns does not of itself establish a partnership.

Q (objective): Receiving a share of the profits of a business is what kind of evidence that the person is a partner? (Two words, Latin.)
Accept: prima facie | prima facie evidence
Hint: It means evidence that holds until disproved.
Why: Art. 1769(4) calls a share of the profits prima facie evidence of partnership.
