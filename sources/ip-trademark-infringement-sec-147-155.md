# Source text
147.1. The owner of a registered mark shall have the exclusive right to prevent all third parties not having the owner's consent from using in the course of trade identical or similar signs or containers for goods or services which are identical or similar to those in respect of which the trademark is registered where such use would result in a likelihood of confusion. In case of the use of an identical sign for identical goods or services, a likelihood of confusion shall be presumed.

Section 155. Remedies; Infringement. - Any person who shall, without the consent of the owner of the registered mark: 155.1. Use in commerce any reproduction, counterfeit, copy, or colorable imitation of a registered mark or the same container or a dominant feature thereof in connection with the sale, offering for sale, distribution, advertising of any goods or services including other preparatory steps necessary to carry out the sale of any goods or services on or in connection with which such use is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive;

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- The owner of a registered mark may stop others, without consent, from using identical or similar signs on identical or similar goods or services when this would likely cause confusion (Sec. 147.1).
- If an identical sign is used for identical goods or services, likelihood of confusion is presumed (Sec. 147.1).
- Infringement includes using in commerce a colorable imitation of a registered mark, or of a dominant feature of it, on goods or services where this is likely to cause confusion, mistake or deception (Sec. 155.1).
- Under the dominancy test, the focus is on the similarity of the dominant features of the competing marks that might cause confusion; exact copying is not required.

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: Sarap Foods registered the mark "LOLA NENA" for bottled sauces. A rival starts selling bottled sauces under "LOLA NENA" with the same label. Must Sarap Foods prove that buyers were actually confused?
Model answer: No. Under Sec. 147.1, when an identical sign is used for identical goods, likelihood of confusion is presumed. The rival uses the identical mark on the same kind of goods without consent, so Sarap Foods need not prove actual confusion; the use is infringement under Sec. 155.1.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Original bar-style problem. A related issue was asked in the 2025 Bar Examination, Commercial Law.) Tasa Corp. owns the registered mark "KAPE KO" for ground coffee, printed in bold red letters over a steaming brown cup. A new company starts selling ground coffee in the same supermarkets under "KAPE KOO", also in bold red letters over a steaming brown cup, with its company name in small print at the bottom. Sued for infringement, the new company argues that the marks are not identical and that its company name on the pack shows the products come from a different maker. Is there infringement? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: Yes. The new company's use of "KAPE KOO" infringes Tasa Corp.'s registered mark.
- Legal basis: Under Sec. 155.1 of the IP Code, using in commerce, without the owner's consent, a colorable imitation of a registered mark or of its dominant feature on goods where it is likely to cause confusion, mistake or deception is infringement. Sec. 147.1 gives the registered owner the right to prevent the use of similar signs on similar goods when this would likely cause confusion. Under the dominancy test, what matters is the similarity of the marks' dominant features, not exact identity.
- Application: The dominant features of Tasa Corp.'s mark are the words "KAPE KO" in bold red letters over a steaming brown cup. "KAPE KOO" copies all of these, with only one extra letter, and is used on the same goods sold in the same stores. An ordinary buyer is likely to be confused. A company name in small print does not remove the confusion created by the dominant features, and the marks need not be identical.
- Conclusion: Because "KAPE KOO" is a colorable imitation of the dominant features of "KAPE KO" on identical goods and is likely to cause confusion, there is trademark infringement under Sec. 155.1.

# Objective and multiple-choice quiz (Year 1-2)
Q (multiple choice): Under Sec. 147.1, when is likelihood of confusion PRESUMED?
A) When a similar sign is used for different goods
B) When an identical sign is used for identical goods or services
C) Whenever the mark is unregistered
D) Never; it must always be proved
Correct: B
Hint: Read the last sentence of Sec. 147.1.
Hint: Both the sign and the goods are the same.
Why: Sec. 147.1 presumes likelihood of confusion when an identical sign is used for identical goods or services.

Q (multiple choice): Under the dominancy test, the court mainly looks at:
A) the whole packaging, including prices and small print
B) the similarity of the marks' dominant features
C) whether the two companies are related
D) the date each company was incorporated
Correct: B
Hint: The test is named after what it focuses on.
Hint: Sec. 155.1 also speaks of "a dominant feature" of the mark.
Why: The dominancy test focuses on similarity of the dominant features of the competing marks that might cause confusion.

Q (objective): A copy of a registered mark that is close enough to deceive, though not exact, is called a ______ imitation. (One word.)

Hint: Sec. 155.1 lists "reproduction, counterfeit, copy, or … imitation".
Why: Sec. 155.1 covers a colorable imitation of a registered mark.

Q (objective): What is the key question in trademark infringement under Secs. 147.1 and 155.1: likelihood of ______? (One word.)

Hint: Both sections use this word.
Why: Infringement turns on whether the use is likely to cause confusion, mistake or deception.
