# Source text
3.1. "Securities" are shares, participation or interests in a corporation or in a commercial enterprise or profit-making venture and evidenced by a certificate, contract, instruments, whether written or electronic in character. It includes: (a) Shares of stocks, bonds, debentures, notes evidences of indebtedness, asset-backed securities; (b) Investment contracts, certificates of interest or participation in a profit sharing agreement, certifies of deposit for a future subscription; (...)

8.1. Securities shall not be sold or offered for sale or distribution within the Philippines, without a registration statement duly filed with and approved by the Commission. Prior to such sale, information on the securities, in such form and with such substance as the Commission may prescribe, shall be made available to each prospective purchaser.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- "Securities" include investment contracts and participation in profit-sharing agreements (Sec. 3.1(b)).
- Howey test for an investment contract: (1) an investment of money, (2) in a common enterprise, (3) with an expectation of profits, (4) arising primarily from the efforts of others.
- Calling the product a "slot", "membership" or "partnership" does not matter; its economic reality decides.
- Securities may not be sold or offered in the Philippines without a registration statement filed with and approved by the SEC (Sec. 8.1), unless exempt.

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: A cooperative-style group sells "rice-farm units" to the public online. Buyers pay P20,000 each; the organisers manage the farms and promise buyers 25% of the harvest income every season. Buyers do no farm work. Is this a security?
Model answer: Yes. It is an investment contract under Sec. 3.1(b). Under the Howey test, buyers invest money in a common enterprise (the pooled farms), expect profits (25% of harvest income), and those profits come from the organisers' efforts, not theirs. So the units are securities that must be registered with the SEC before being offered (Sec. 8.1).

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Original bar-style problem. A related issue was asked in the 2016 Bar Examination, Commercial Law.) Kawan Livestock Inc. advertised on social media "cattle-raising slots" at P50,000 each. Buyers do nothing: Kawan buys, raises and sells the cattle, then pays each slot holder a fixed 30% return after six months. Kawan never filed a registration statement with the SEC. When the SEC issued a cease-and-desist order, Kawan argued that it sells livestock-raising services, not shares of stock, so the Securities Regulation Code does not apply. Is Kawan correct? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: No. Kawan is selling securities in the form of investment contracts, and it needed to register them with the SEC.
- Legal basis: Under Sec. 3.1(b) of the Securities Regulation Code, securities include investment contracts and participation in profit-sharing agreements. Under the Howey test, an investment contract exists where there is an investment of money, in a common enterprise, with an expectation of profits, primarily from the efforts of others. Under Sec. 8.1, securities may not be sold or offered in the Philippines without a registration statement filed with and approved by the SEC.
- Application: Buyers pay P50,000 each (an investment of money) into Kawan's cattle-raising business, which pools their money (a common enterprise). They expect a 30% return (an expectation of profits), which comes entirely from Kawan's buying, raising and selling of cattle (the efforts of others). All the elements are present, so the slots are investment contracts, whatever Kawan calls them. Kawan offered them to the public without a registration statement.
- Conclusion: The cattle-raising slots are securities, their sale without SEC registration violates Sec. 8.1, and the SEC's cease-and-desist order is proper.
